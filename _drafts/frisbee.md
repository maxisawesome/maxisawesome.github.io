Few people push their bodies for the sake of amateur competition the way that I have done so for Ultimate Frisbee.  I have tried out for teams, gone to tournaments, practiced at least once a week, and trained explicitly for this sport well beyond my college career. As anyone in tech will tell you, you learn a lot by doing hard things, and doing hard things in a group teaches you a lot about how to execute in a group. 
A lot of these lessons from over the years came back to me again. All of them branch out to non-frisbee activities. If it's a lesson about teamwork, it extends to teamwork at your startup. If it's a lesson about improving at a skill, it extends to coding or learning an instrument. Some are already at meta level one above frisbee itself. 

### The rules, quickly ✅

Little of the wisdom within these lessons requires an understanding of the actual rules of Ultimate, but it's good context, and given how infrequently I get the opportunity to opine on them, I'm going to teach you.

Ultimate is not a complicated game. You can't run with the disc. The field is 70 yards long, 40 yards wide, with two 20 yard end zones (an American football field with endzones twice as big, a correspondingly shorter play area, and slightly thinner). There are seven players on the field for each team and everyone comes off the field after each point. Catching the disc in the endzone gets you one point. Games are played to a score, usually 13 or 15. One team is on offense, the other is on defense, and if the disc hits the ground or goes out of bounds, this swaps without a stoppage of play.

## It's easier to make a team better by adding someone new who is more skilled than it is to make someone who's already on the team better.  ✅

Getting better at something is difficult. It is hard in frisbee because you don't have that many reps, they are costly, and learning physical skills is sample inefficient. There are many underdog stories of the team getting together, grinding it out, and overcoming the odds to get good enough to beat the big guy. These are impactful and endearing, but unrealistic. By far the easiest way to improve a group that is doing something is to add a new member (and maybe replace an old one). New thoughts and skills will subsequently uplevel everyone else much faster - it's also easier to arrive to a higher skill level that is already known than it is to create new skill in a way that is not previously understood.

## One tournament is worth about 8-10 weeks worth of practices ✅
Practice is good for targeting specific skills, but two days of actually hammering your skills against opponents who are trying their best to exploit your weaknesses is way more valuable. There is something more spiritually significant about real reps versus practice reps that make them more impactful.

## Everything is about percentages. Really good players don't mess up. 
Frisbee is played "on serve" - if you do everything correctly, the offense wins. Skilled handlers (people who are more responsible for throwing the disc) can control the way the disc flies through the air well enough to make defense more or less impossible. As such, if you start with the disc, you should win that point. 

This means that in high level ultimate, one or two mistakes will change the course of the game. It comes down to "who can make the fewest mistakes" and "who can capitalize on their opponent's mistakes".  The skill is "don't mess up the small things ever" combined with "make something out of nothing a few times per game." 

## Making opportunities vs easy opportunities 
There are two types of Ds you can get in frisbee (a D is when you are on defense and you knock the disc to the ground or catch it, putting your team on offense and giving you a chance to score). The first are the type where you _should_ get the disc. Something like: the disc isn't thrown as directly at the person you're defending as it should have been, you get between the disc and the person you're defending, and you have an easy play. An opportunity is given to you based on another's mistake and simple execution is the only thing needed to complete it. 

The other type of D is one where you _create_ an opportunity. You lag a little behind your man to make him look like he's open when you know you're fast enough to catch up, baiting a throw you get a block on. You notice another defender is getting beat deep, and your help defense saves the day in the end zone. Being able to generate something versus taking what's offered is the difference between a player that is filling his role and one that is running the game. These players are extremely valuable.

## You gotta layout once before you're ready to layout. ✅
"Laying out" in frisbee means diving for the disc. It's usually only worth it if there's a small window that you need to get your hand into a place to make a play. During a tournament, when you get the first opportunity to layout for a disc, by the time you realize you have an opportunity to layout, it's too late. You can't realize and then do it, you just need to do it. That extra cycle will miss your chance. In order to not miss the next one, you need to layout anyway. In that first opportunity, you realize you have a chance, realize you're too late already, then do it regardless. This'll prime your body for the next chance you get, and the recognition moment won't be necessary, you'll just pull the trigger.

## Mentality and mood is half the battle 
When I was a captain in college, easily the most important thing I did as a leader was manage the team's emotions. I'm fine and good enough at the frisbee strategy, but I improved our gameplay significantly more by making sure everyone was eating, wearing sunscreen, hydrating, and staying upbeat. Frisbee is very momentum based and if you're pissed off you play worse. 

## Skill gives you de facto leadership ✅
If you're good, people will look up to you. It's irrelevant whether or not you have good leadership skills or good decision making abilities or competency in people management, your group will look to you for guidance. Use this if you so desire. I'd argue that if you are good because you want to do well, then you want the team to do well, then this is an advantage you need to make use of. You see this everywhere - a good IC is more likely to be promoted to manager, even though the skillsets are completely separate. If you are good, you should make the choice between embracing this or actively moving away from it.

## Leadership shines mostly when it's individually targeted. ✅
Your effectiveness as a leader goes up significantly if you tailor it to each individual person. Some people need encouragement after they make a mistake, some people need harsh words, some people need five minutes alone. Each person would suffer under another's optimal strategy. Don't lose yourself but tailor your style to the individuals you're leading.

## The easiest way to boost morale is to make a cool play ✅
If everyone is down and you're losing, by far the simplest way to turn things around is to do something insanely sick as fuck. Make a diving block or a shoestring score. Toe-tap the line at the back of the endzone. Make your defender fall on his ass with a great cut. The whole team will be hyped in an instant. It is also your responsibility, if you are on the sideline, to freak tf out on how cool the play was and what a badass your teammate is. That is your role to play in that play.

## There is no "should have won that game" ✅
Frisbee is not huge, so you end up playing the same teams tournament over tournament and year over year. Everyone in a region gets sifted into their respective rankings on the tier list and, for the most part, stay there. It's a common thing to be driving to a tournament, chatting with your teammates about the last game you played against whomever you're up against that day, and hear something along the lines of "Gah, we nearly had that! We should have won that game." 

You need to understand that "should have won that game" is devoid of meaning. If you should have won, then you would have played better. Both teams played at whatever level they did and one came out the victor. They had the higher score, so they should have won. There is [no counterfactual in this life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unbearable_Lightness_of_Being). Very rarely in sports do you actually get the benefit of a five game series to find out who's statistically best. You only get one opportunity and it is the evaluation.


## There's plenty of glory in individual plays even if you're losing the battle. ✅
You're not gonna win every game but execution to the highest order, giving a point your all, or a layout block when you're down by 5, has glory in and of itself. It doesn't have to lead to the win for it to give you pride.

## Pressure is a privilege ✅
Most people play (or work, or move through life) without much at stake. If the stakes are high and the pressure is on, it's because in that moment you're doing something worthwhile. Emotions are a radar, and (properly aligned) stress is a signal that what you're doing has meaning.

## It's really fun to move your body ✅
Occasionally I meet someone who's got this "my body is a meatsack for my mind" mentality. There are fewer statements I disagree with more vehemently. To be human is to be embodied. To engage that body and expand, exercise, and utilize it is a spiritual experience. My universe is my existence and I owe it to that universe to stretch it to its limits.  

## You're not all that conscious of your body all the time ✅
This is more of an observation, but I'm constantly amused by how, no matter the sport nor the obviousness of a foul, everyone is shocked that they're getting called for a foul. It's some sort of testament to how not-conscious you are of many parts of your body during exercise. 

## please play team sports
Doing stuff together with people is good. It is cool and fun and teaches you so much and is so rewarding. Others seem to be able to train purely for the sake of training, but I can train for a goal (jumping higher, running fast in frisbee) much more easily than I'm able to do so for a vague goal of general health.
